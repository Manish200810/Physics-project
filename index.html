02 · Method one

🌑 Shadow Method

The first idea was to let light draw the geometry for us.

01
Physics principle — rectilinear propagation of light. Light travels approximately in straight lines in a uniform medium. A controlled shadow therefore preserves geometric information about the object's boundaries. With known reference distances and similar-triangle relationships, those projected dimensions can be related to the glass slab.

Apparatus

Glass slab · controlled/point light source · screen or surface · stand/holder · metre scale or known reference.

What we observe

We observe the shadow boundaries and the known geometry of the light-source arrangement rather than simply measuring the slab face.

Procedure

  1. Fix the glass slab and light source.
  2. Produce a clear shadow on the screen/surface.
  3. Mark the relevant shadow boundaries.
  4. Measure the known reference distances.
  5. Use the corresponding geometric ratios to infer dimensions.
  6. Repeat the observation to improve reliability.
LengthUse the shadow dimension corresponding to the longer side of the glass slab and the known setup geometry.
BreadthUse the perpendicular projected dimension with the same geometric relationship.
Width / ThicknessUse the relevant projected edge/displacement associated with the slab thickness in the chosen setup.
dimension ratio = corresponding shadow/reference ratio The exact final working equation should match the distances and geometry of our actual setup.

03 · Method two

👁️ Parallax Method

Next, we changed the observer instead of the glass slab.

02
Physics principle — parallax. When the observer changes position, an object appears to shift relative to references. By controlling the observer position and measuring the apparent displacement/alignment change, the geometry of the glass slab can be inferred.

Apparatus

Glass slab · reference markers/pins · scale · stable setup · controlled observation positions.

Key idea

The slab stays fixed. The viewpoint changes, and that change in viewpoint becomes measurable information.

Procedure

  1. Fix the slab and reference markers.
  2. Observe from one marked position.
  3. Move to another controlled position.
  4. Record the apparent displacement or alignment change.
  5. Use the viewing geometry to infer the required dimension.
  6. Repeat for consistency.
LengthUse parallax between appropriate end points of the glass slab along its longer direction.
BreadthRepeat the same geometric construction perpendicular to the length.
Width / ThicknessUse the apparent displacement between appropriate front/rear or upper/lower edges to infer thickness.
observer displacement → apparent displacement → geometry → physical dimension The precise equation depends on the reference positions and distances in our actual setup.

04 · Method three

📷 Photography / Pixel Method

Then we stopped measuring the slab directly and measured its image.

03
Physics principle — image calibration. A digital image is made of pixels. If a known physical reference appears in the same image, its pixel length establishes a scale. That scale can be applied to the corresponding pixel dimension of the glass slab.

Apparatus

Glass slab · phone/camera · known reference scale/object · stable surface · suitable lighting.

Basic relationship

scale = actual reference size ÷ reference pixels

dimension = slab pixels × scale

Procedure

  1. Place the glass slab beside a known reference.
  2. Photograph them in the same effective measurement plane.
  3. Measure the reference in pixels.
  4. Measure the slab dimension in pixels.
  5. Apply the calibration scale.
  6. Repeat for length, breadth and thickness where the geometry permits.
LengthCount the pixel distance between the two ends and convert it using the calibrated scale.
BreadthMeasure the perpendicular pixel distance between the corresponding side edges.
Width / ThicknessMeasure the visible thickness in an image where the camera angle and reference geometry make that dimension resolvable.
scale = known physical dimension / corresponding pixel dimension

measured slab dimension = slab pixel dimension × scale Perspective, camera tilt, lens distortion and uncertain edge pixels can affect accuracy.

05 · Method four

🔬 Optical / Refraction Method

Finally, we made the glass slab reveal itself through the path of light.

04
Physics principle — refraction. When light moves between media with different refractive indices, its direction changes. For a plane-parallel glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced. That optical behaviour can be related to the slab geometry.

Apparatus

Glass slab · ray box/controlled light source · paper/screen · pins or markers as required · protractor · scale.

Core law

n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

Procedure

  1. Set the glass slab in the chosen ray geometry.
  2. Mark incident and emergent directions.
  3. Measure the relevant angles or displacement.
  4. Apply the optical relation required by the setup.
  5. Repeat with controlled geometry.
  6. Use the construction to infer length, breadth and thickness.
LengthUse an optical construction aligned with the longer dimension and the measured displacement/geometry.
BreadthRepeat the construction perpendicular to the length direction.
Width / ThicknessRelate the lateral displacement and refraction geometry to the slab thickness.
For a plane-parallel slab: d = t · sin(i − r) / cos r Here t represents slab thickness. The exact working equation must match the actual ray geometry used.

07 · The plot twist

And then… we tried free fall.

This is where the neat theory met an imperfect experiment.

⚠️ The method that didn't cooperate

We attempted a free-fall approach in which a falling object and timing information would be used for indirect measurement. The equations looked perfectly reasonable. Our setup was not quite as cooperative.

Why it failed

  • Finite size of the ball: release and impact were not ideal point events.
  • Release uncertainty: even a small initial push affects the motion.
  • Timing error: short time intervals are difficult to measure manually.
  • Alignment: the geometry needs to match the assumptions of the model.
  • Measurement uncertainty: small setup errors can become noticeable in the final calculation.

What we learned

A method can be mathematically correct and still be experimentally weak. The real experiment is the equation plus a setup capable of satisfying the assumptions behind that equation.

Experimental lesson: good physics is not only about the formula. It is also about controlling uncertainty.
