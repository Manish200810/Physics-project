01

Shadow Method

Our first idea was simple: if the slab can create a measurable projection, perhaps its shadow can reveal its dimensions.

Physics Principle

Light propagates approximately in straight lines through a uniform medium. When a glass slab is placed in the path of light, its outline can produce a projection. By comparing this projected dimension with a known reference and using geometrical similarity, the unknown dimension can be estimated.

Apparatus

  • Rectangular glass slab
  • Light source
  • Screen
  • Reference object
  • Metre scale
  • Stand/support

Procedure

  1. Place the glass slab in a fixed position.
  2. Illuminate the slab with a suitable light source.
  3. Obtain a clear projection.
  4. Introduce a reference dimension.
  5. Measure the relevant projected distances.
  6. Use the geometrical relationship to determine the dimension.
  7. Repeat for the required orientations.

What are we measuring?

The important idea is not the shadow itself, but the relationship between the actual object, the reference and their projected sizes.

Length

The longitudinal extent of the projected slab can be compared with a known reference length.

Breadth

The transverse extent of the projection can similarly be calibrated.

Thickness

A suitable orientation allows the thickness to contribute a measurable projected dimension.

Unknown dimension ≈ Known reference dimension × (Projected slab dimension ÷ Projected reference dimension)

02

Optical Method

Next, we moved from ordinary projection to image formation and geometrical optics.

Physics Principle

A convex lens forms an image of an object. By arranging the lens, glass slab and screen appropriately, the effect of inserting the slab can be studied through changes in image position or optical geometry. The relevant distances can then be related to the slab dimension.

Apparatus

  • Convex lens
  • Glass slab
  • Screen
  • Scale
  • Object
  • Optical bench/support

Procedure

  1. Set up the convex lens and screen.
  2. Obtain a sharp image.
  3. Record the relevant optical positions.
  4. Introduce the glass slab.
  5. Observe the change in the optical arrangement.
  6. Measure the required distances.
  7. Use the chosen optical relation to obtain the thickness.

Why optics?

The slab interacts with light through refraction, allowing its physical thickness to be investigated through an optical displacement.

Length

The physical outline of the slab can be established using the optical setup and its geometrical reference positions.

Breadth

The corresponding transverse geometry can be established from the traced or referenced optical arrangement.

Thickness

The thickness is obtained from the optical displacement produced by the slab in the chosen setup.

For refraction: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

For a plane-parallel slab: d = t · sin(i − r) / cos r

03

Photography / Pixel Method

Then we realised that a photograph isn't just a picture—it is a coordinate system made of pixels.

Physics Principle

A digital image is composed of pixels. If a reference object of known physical dimension appears in the same image plane as the glass slab, its pixel dimension can establish a scale factor. That scale can then be applied to the slab's pixel dimensions.

Scale = Actual reference size ÷ Reference size in pixels

Slab dimension = Slab dimension in pixels × Scale

Apparatus

  • Glass slab
  • Smartphone/camera
  • Reference object
  • Stable flat surface
  • Pixel measurement application

Procedure

  1. Place a known reference beside the slab.
  2. Keep both in approximately the same plane.
  3. Take a photograph from a suitable position.
  4. Measure the reference in pixels.
  5. Measure the slab in pixels.
  6. Calculate the scale factor.
  7. Convert the dimensions into physical units.

Important Points

  • Keep the camera perpendicular.
  • Avoid perspective distortion.
  • Use sharp edges.
  • Keep the reference close to the slab.
  • Repeat measurements.

Length

Measure the slab's length in pixels and convert it using the reference scale.

Breadth

Measure the transverse pixel distance and apply the same calibration.

Thickness

Use a suitable front/side view so the thickness can be resolved in pixels.

04

Parallax Method

Our fourth approach changed the observer rather than the object.

Physics Principle

Parallax is the apparent displacement of an object when it is viewed from different positions. The amount of apparent displacement depends on the geometry of the object, observer and baseline. By controlling these quantities, dimensional information can be obtained indirectly.

Apparatus

  • Glass slab
  • Camera/eye
  • Reference object
  • Known baseline
  • Scale
  • Stable support

Procedure

  1. Fix the glass slab.
  2. Place a suitable reference object.
  3. Observe from the first position.
  4. Record the apparent position.
  5. Move through a known baseline.
  6. Record the second apparent position.
  7. Measure the displacement.
  8. Apply the appropriate geometry.

Key Concept

The slab remains fixed. The observer changes position, producing an apparent shift that contains geometrical information.

Length

The apparent positions of relevant longitudinal edges can be used to establish the length through calibrated geometry.

Breadth

A suitable lateral viewing geometry allows the breadth to be inferred.

Thickness

The apparent displacement between surfaces or edges can provide information about thickness.

Parallax angle θ ≈ apparent displacement ÷ observation distance

The final dimensional equation depends on the exact experimental geometry used.

05 · The failed attempt

Then came free fall. 😭

After four different approaches, we also considered a free-fall based method. The basic idea was to use a falling object and timing measurements to obtain information indirectly.

But there was one problem:

We couldn't find the right ball.

The object needed to be suitable for a controlled and repeatable release. Without an appropriate ball, the experimental setup became difficult to standardise.

  • The ball has a finite size, making the exact release position uncertain.
  • The ball may not be released from exactly the same point each time.
  • Short fall times make manual timing particularly sensitive to reaction time.
  • Small changes in alignment affect the measured distance.
  • Air resistance and the actual motion of the object can introduce additional differences from an ideal model.
  • Repeated trials would be necessary to distinguish random variation from systematic error.

07 · Error Analysis

How close were our methods?

A measurement is never complete without discussing uncertainty. Percentage error gives us a way to express the difference between a measured value and a chosen reference value relative to that reference.

Percentage Error

Percentage Error = |Measured value − Reference value| ÷ Reference value × 100

For comparison, if the Shadow Method values (L = 8.46 cm, B = 6.05 cm and t = 1.83 cm) are taken as a reference set, the other recorded results can be compared against them. This is a comparison between our methods, not a claim that the shadow method is an absolute standard.

Dimension Reference Photography Parallax
Length 8.46 cm ≈ 5.67% ≈ 0.47%
Breadth 6.05 cm ≈ 14.88% ≈ 0.83%
Thickness 1.83 cm ≈ 26.23% 0%

What do these percentages tell us?

The percentages show that different measurement techniques can respond very differently to alignment, calibration, perspective, edge identification and other experimental uncertainties. A small percentage difference does not automatically prove a method is perfect, while a larger difference does not necessarily mean the physics is wrong. It can indicate that the particular setup requires better calibration, alignment or repeated trials.

And then there was the Tape Measure App.

After exploring shadows, optics, photography and parallax, we eventually looked at how modern smartphones approach the same basic problem: converting information captured by a camera into physical dimensions.

1. Camera Geometry

The phone camera captures a two-dimensional image of the real world. From the way objects appear in the image, geometric relationships can be used to estimate distances and dimensions.

2. Motion & Sensors

Modern phones contain motion sensors such as accelerometers and gyroscopes. These can help determine how the device moves and changes orientation while measurements are being made.

3. Spatial Mapping

Computer-vision systems can identify surfaces, edges and spatial relationships. By combining camera information with estimated three-dimensional geometry, the application can convert image-space measurements into real-world distances.

4. Calibration

The fundamental idea is similar to our photography method: image information needs a relationship to physical space. Instead of manually placing a reference object and counting pixels, the phone estimates scale using its camera and spatial information.

5. Computer Vision

The software analyses visual features such as edges, planes and reference points. Tracking these features while the phone moves lets the system estimate where surfaces exist in three-dimensional space.

6. The Modern Version

In other words, the underlying idea is still physics plus geometry— but the calculations, calibration and spatial tracking are performed automatically by the phone.

What took us multiple setups, measurements and a considerable amount of alignment…

…can now be done with a phone. 📱
